\begin{abstract}
How much information about a latent variable survives a frozen generative model is
usually measured through the performance of a particular encoder, inversion method or
decoder. This conflates what the channel preserves with what a chosen receiver can
recover. We introduce FIBER, a framework that characterises latent recoverability as a
direction-resolved Bayesian observability geometry. For a latent $Z$ and an observation
$Y$ we define $\Cobs=\Cov(\Exp[Z\mid Y])$, whose spectrum orders latent directions by how
much posterior uncertainty the observation removes. Because the Bayes decoder is
unavailable, we derive a decoder-certified operator $\Ccert(f)\preceq\Cobs$ together with
a cross-fitted estimation procedure, so a learned observer supplies a lower certificate
rather than an unconstrained proxy. In a linear--Gaussian channel with known ground truth,
FIBER recovers the analytic observability spectrum and exposes a
\emph{sensitivity--observability mismatch}: directions that perturb the output more can
nevertheless be less recoverable. On a frozen text-to-image diffusion channel, however,
the certified estimator establishes no positive observable direction at the tested scale,
and a preregistered receiver-side-information intervention finds no detectable recovery
benefit from sample-specific conditioning, while both the architecture and
conditioning-marginal placebo gates pass. These results separate what can be certified
about a generative channel from what a trained receiver merely appears to recover, and
expose practical limits of latent observability in modern generative systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A frozen generative model induces a channel from its latent input to its observable
output. Once the weights, the sampler and the conditioning protocol are fixed, an initial
latent $Z$ is transformed into an image $X$, which may be further altered by compression,
resizing, noise or other transmission effects before a receiver observes $Y$. Recovering
information about $Z$ from $Y$ underlies diffusion inversion, latent-space watermarking,
provenance, and other applications that transmit information through generated content.
Existing methods have accordingly developed increasingly sophisticated latent patterns,
embedding regions, coding schemes, inversion procedures and learned decoders, and they
answer an important engineering question: \emph{how should information be encoded so that
it can later be recovered?}

\paragraph{Where does ``reliable'' come from?}
Recent diffusion-native watermarking already provides strong evidence that latent
transmission is highly nonuniform. Tree-Ring builds robust patterns in selected Fourier
coefficients of the initial noise \citep{wen2023treering}; T2SMark concentrates bits in
empirically reliable tail regions of the latent distribution \citep{t2smark2025}; MaxMark
places enhanced messages in reliable latent regions \citep{chang2026maxmark}. Their
successes are evidence \emph{for} the premise of this paper, not against it: latent
coordinates are plainly not equally useful for transmission. What remains missing is that
``reliable'' is defined through a particular embedding and extraction pipeline. When one
latent pattern is recovered better than another, it is not clear whether the improvement
reflects a more observable direction of the channel or a better match between that
direction and the chosen encoding. \textbf{Can reliability be defined before an encoding
is chosen?}

\paragraph{Was the information lost, or did the receiver fail?}
Inversion-based methods recover their payload by first reconstructing the initial noise
from the generated image \citep{wen2023treering,yang2024gaussianshading}, so measured
robustness depends on the receiver as much as on the channel. This dependence is not
negligible: improving the inversion algorithm alone reduces initial-noise reconstruction
error and, by itself, improves invisible-watermark discrimination
\citep{hong2024exactinversion}. An aggregate recovery score therefore cannot distinguish
information the channel destroyed from information the receiver merely failed to decode,
and cannot say whether recoverability is concentrated in a few latent directions or spread
thin. \textbf{Operational recovery is not channel observability.}

\paragraph{Each advance repairs a different layer.}
The literature has addressed distinct failure modes at distinct layers of the transmission
stack: Gaussian Shading removes training-induced quality costs
\citep{yang2024gaussianshading}, T2SMark trades robustness against latent diversity
\citep{t2smark2025}, MaxMark addresses limited payload capacity
\citep{chang2026maxmark}, Gaussian Shannon adds coding for bit-exact transmission
\citep{zhang2026gaussianshannon}, and GROW removes the computational cost of
inversion-based extraction \citep{luo2026grow}. These are real improvements to
watermarking systems. They also make a measured recovery rate a joint function of the
latent embedding, the channel, the inversion procedure, the coding scheme and the
receiver. \emph{Our inference, and not a claim made by those works}, is that a layer of
abstraction is missing beneath them: before optimising the communication system, one
should be able to characterise the information-carrying geometry of the frozen channel
itself.

We formalise this through the \emph{Bayesian observability geometry}
\[
  \Cobs \;=\; \Cov\bigl(\Exp[Z\mid Y]\bigr).
\]
For an isotropic Gaussian latent and a unit direction $v$,
$v^\top\Cobs v=1-\Exp[\Var(v^\top Z\mid Y)]$, so each quadratic form measures how much
uncertainty about that latent direction the observation removes. The eigenspectrum
separates highly observable directions from directions whose latent variation is largely
lost: its trace summarises \emph{total explained latent variance}, while the full operator
retains \emph{where} that observability lies. This posterior-mean covariance has classical
connections to inverse regression, and in the linear--Gaussian setting it is a bounded
global Bayesian counterpart of the local Fisher geometry.

The Bayes posterior mean is unavailable in a realistic generative channel, which makes the
geometry impossible to compute directly, and replacing it by the output of a learned
decoder is unsafe: $\Cov(f(Y))$ can reflect the decoder's own scaling and estimation error
rather than information the channel carries. We instead derive a \emph{decoder-certified
observability operator}
\[
  \Ccert(f)\;=\;\Cobs-\Cov(m-f)\;\preceq\;\Cobs,\qquad m(Y)=\Exp[Z\mid Y],
\]
so a decoder cannot certify observability beyond the Bayes quantity. This converts a
learned observer from an optimistic proxy into a lower certificate, and motivates
estimating it with disjoint fitting and evaluation data so that decoder fitting noise is
not read back as channel structure.

Finally, observability is not a property of the channel alone. A receiver that observes
$Y$ and one that additionally knows side information $S$ solve different inference
problems. Writing $\Cobs(\mathcal I_R)=\Cov(\Exp[Z\mid\mathcal I_R])$ for a receiver with
information set $\mathcal I_R$, the tower property implies
\[
  \Cobs(Y)\;\preceq\;\Cobs(Y,S),
\]
so additional receiver information can only enlarge the observable latent geometry, and
may do so directionally. \textbf{Observability is thus a property of a channel--receiver
pair.} We test this prediction in a frozen text-to-image diffusion channel with a
preregistered four-arm intervention that separates the correct sample-specific
conditioning from architectural capacity and from the conditioning marginal.

Taken together, the three questions above are the same question asked of three different
confounds, and they fix what this paper is for:
\textbf{we separate channel observability from the choice of encoding, from the quality of
the decoder, and from the knowledge of the receiver.} This is not another watermarking
method; it is an attempt to decompose a quantity --- ``recoverability'' --- that is
currently measured with all three folded in.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A receiver-conditional Bayesian geometry of latent observability.} We
  formulate which latent directions survive a frozen generative channel through
  $\Cobs(\mathcal I_R)$, connecting posterior uncertainty, directional recoverability, and
  classical inverse-regression and Fisher perspectives.
  \item \textbf{A decoder-certified observability operator.} We derive
  $\Ccert(f)\preceq\Cobs$, which lets a learned observer provide a lower certificate
  rather than an unconstrained proxy, together with a cross-fitted finite-sample
  estimation protocol.
  \item \textbf{A monotonicity principle for receiver information.} We show that enriching
  the receiver's information set can only increase population observability, and prove why
  differences of learned decoder certificates do \emph{not} automatically inherit that
  guarantee.
  \item \textbf{Evidence.} \textbf{[Phase A pending.]}
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Posterior-mean geometry has classical roots in inverse regression and Bayesian
dimension reduction.}
Sliced inverse regression \citep{li1991sir} takes the leading eigenvectors of
$\Cov(\Exp[x\mid y])$ as directions of the central subspace, and likelihood-informed
subspaces \citep{cui2014lis} identify the parameter directions along which data update a
prior in nonlinear inverse problems. \textbf{The covariance form is not new here, and we
do not claim it.} What differs is the object it is asked about and the regime it must be
estimated in. Two features of ours invert the classical situation: the condition SIR
requires of the predictor, that its conditional means be linear, holds \emph{exactly and
by construction} because the sampler draws $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ --- an assumption there,
a fact here --- while SIR estimates $\Exp[x\mid y]$ by slicing on a low-dimensional
response, and our conditioning variable is an entire image, so slicing is unavailable. The
inverse-regression curve must therefore be approximated by a trained decoder, which is
precisely why that approximation has to be a \emph{certificate} rather than a proxy
(\S\ref{sec:certificate}).

\paragraph{Information theory characterises informativeness, but typically not its latent
directional structure under a specified receiver.}
Mutual information and MMSE are tightly linked in Gaussian channels
\citep{guo2005immse}; Fisher information measures local distinguishability; and
Blackwell's comparison of experiments \citep{blackwell1953} formalises when one
observation structure is more informative than another. Each answers a question we need
and none answers ours: mutual information returns a scalar, Fisher geometry is local, and
Blackwell's ordering tells us that $(Y,S)$ is at least as informative as $Y$ without
saying \emph{which latent directions} the extra information acts on. We keep the
direction structure and make the receiver explicit, writing
$\Cobs(\mathcal I_R)=\Cov(\Exp[Z\mid\mathcal I_R])$, so that ``how much survives'' and
``which directions are observable, and to whom'' are separate questions with separate
answers. Fisher information appears here only as a bridge
(Proposition~\ref{prop:fisher}) locating a global Bayesian quantity against a familiar
local one, and we claim no novelty for the principle that more receiver information cannot
hurt Bayes estimation.

\paragraph{Diffusion inversion and watermarking optimise recovery and embedding; we first
ask which latent directions are observable at all.}
Latent-space methods design payloads, robust patterns, reliable regions or inversion
procedures to improve recovery from generated images: Tree-Ring places a pattern in a
Fourier region of the initial noise \citep{wen2023treering}, Gaussian Shading preserves
the latent's Gaussian law \citep{yang2024gaussianshading}, Stable Signature roots a
decodable signature in the decoder \citep{fernandez2023stablesignature}, and recent work
treats generation explicitly as a noisy channel and applies coding
\citep{zhang2026gaussianshannon,chang2026maxmark}. Work on inverting DDIM sampling
reports that accurate image reconstruction does not imply accurate recovery of the initial
latent \citep{zeng2026inverting}, a separation our object is built to express. These
methods primarily optimise \emph{how} information should be embedded or recovered; they do
not define and certify a receiver-dependent observability operator of the underlying frozen
channel. We therefore ask a prior question: \emph{before choosing an encoding, which latent
directions does the frozen generative channel make recoverable, and to which receiver?}

\section{Method}

\subsection{Bayesian observability geometry of frozen generative channels}
\label{sec:geometry}

\emph{What is observable?}

Let $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d)$ be the initial latent, $S$ the conditioning actually supplied
to the generator, $X=G(Z,S)$ the produced image with $G$ frozen, and $Y=T(X)$ the
observation after a transmission channel $T$ (compression, resizing, noise, blur). The
receiver sees $Y$ and nothing else unless stated otherwise.

\begin{definition}[Observability operator]
$\Cobs=\Cov\bigl(\Exp[Z\mid Y]\bigr)$.
\end{definition}

Its quadratic form is the quantity of interest:
$v^\top\Cobs v=\Var\bigl(\Exp[v^\top Z\mid Y]\bigr)$, and by the law of total variance,
for $\|v\|=1$ and $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$,
\begin{equation}
  v^\top\Cobs v \;=\; 1-\Exp\bigl[\Var(v^\top Z\mid Y)\bigr] \;\in\;[0,1].
  \label{eq:quadform}
\end{equation}
So $v^\top\Cobs v$ is the fraction of the prior uncertainty in direction $v$ that an
observation removes: $1$ means $v^\top Z$ is recoverable, $0$ means the channel destroyed
it. Writing $\Cobs=V\Lambda V^\top$, eigenvectors are observable latent directions,
eigenvalues are direction-wise observability strengths, and the top-$k$ eigenspace is the
most observable $k$-dimensional latent subspace. Figure~\ref{fig:geometry} is this
definition and nothing else.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{fig_geometry.tex}
\caption{Observability is anisotropic even when the prior is not. (a) Every unit direction
starts with the same prior variance. (b) Observing $Y$ narrows the posterior by different
amounts in different directions; dashed grey is the prior, solid colour the posterior.
(c) Collecting those numbers gives $\Cobs$, whose principal directions are $v_j$, with directional observability strengths
$\lambda_j=1-\Exp[\Var(v_j^\top Z\mid Y)]$. The trace summarises how much latent
variance is observable; the spectrum reveals where that observability lies.}
\label{fig:geometry}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Relation to Fisher information.}
The definition is global and Bayesian; the familiar local object is not. The two agree in
the linear--Gaussian case, which locates $\Cobs$ rather than replacing it.

\begin{proposition}[Fisher bridge]
\label{prop:fisher}
Let $Y=AZ+\varepsilon$ with $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma_
\varepsilon)$ independent, and let $J_F=A^\top\Sigma_\varepsilon^{-1}A$ be the Fisher
information of $Y$ about $Z$. Then
\[
  \Cobs \;=\; J_F\,(I+J_F)^{-1},
\]
so $\Cobs$ and $J_F$ share eigenvectors and $\lambda_{\mathrm{obs},j}=\lambda_{F,j}/(1+
\lambda_{F,j})$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The posterior precision is $I+J_F$, so $\Cov(Z\mid Y)=(I+J_F)^{-1}$ and
$\Cobs=\Cov(Z)-\Exp[\Cov(Z\mid Y)]=I-(I+J_F)^{-1}=J_F(I+J_F)^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

Fisher information asks whether two nearby latent states are locally distinguishable;
$\Cobs$ asks how much of a direction is globally inferable under the prior. The map
$\mu\mapsto\mu/(1+\mu)$ compresses an unbounded local quantity into $[0,1)$, which is
exactly the population range implied by \eqref{eq:quadform}.

\subsection{Decoder-certified observability}
\label{sec:certificate}

\emph{How can observability be measured without knowing the Bayes decoder?}

$m(Y)=\Exp[Z\mid Y]$ is unavailable, so let $f$ be a decoder trained to approximate it.
The natural substitute $\Cov(f(Y))$ is not usable: it is not a lower bound on $\Cobs$ for
an approximate decoder, and its error grows with the decoder's scale rather than with its
quality. We use instead, with $z_c,f_c$ the centred latent and decoder output,
\begin{equation}
  \Ccert(f)\;=\;\Exp\bigl[z_cf_c^\top+f_cz_c^\top-f_cf_c^\top\bigr].
  \label{eq:ccert}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Certificate]
\label{thm:cert}
For any square-integrable $f$, $\ \Ccert(f)=\Cobs-\Cov(m-f)\preceq\Cobs$, with equality
iff $m-f$ is almost surely constant.
\end{theorem}

\noindent\textbf{Consequence.} Every direction $\Ccert$ reports is one the channel really
carries. Reducing the decoder-error covariance in the Loewner order raises the
certificate, and no decoder can certify beyond $\Cobs$; a smaller mean squared error alone
constrains the trace and does not order the operators. $\Ccert$ is also invariant to a
constant shift of $f$, which is why equality is stated on $m-f$ up to a constant rather
than on $f=m$.

\paragraph{$\Ccert$ is indefinite, and that is the point.}
$\Cov(f)$ is positive semidefinite by construction --- which is precisely why it cannot be
a bound. $\Ccert$ can have negative eigenvalues: since
$v^\top\Ccert v=v^\top\Cobs v-\Var(v^\top(m-f))$, a negative direction is one where the
decoder's error exceeds the true observable variation. Two consequences follow and both are easy to get
wrong. First, any solver that ranks by $|\lambda|$ --- every SVD-based routine --- returns
the directions the decoder is \emph{worst} on as the leading ones; the eigensolver must be
algebraically largest. Second, $\Ccert\preceq\Cobs$ orders quadratic forms, not volumes:
drawing the certificate as a smaller ellipsoid nested inside $\Cobs$ is wrong whenever
$\Ccert$ has a negative direction. Figure~\ref{fig:certificate} draws it as a signed
spectrum instead.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{fig_certificate.tex}
\caption{The certificate is not a smaller ellipsoid. (a) $\Cobs$ is positive semidefinite
with eigenvalues in $[0,1]$, so it has an ellipsoid. (b) $\Ccert(f)$ is indefinite: its
positive part is the certified observability mass $\Dcert$, and a negative direction is one
where decoder error exceeds the observable variation. $\Ccert\preceq\Cobs$ is an ordering of quadratic forms,
so nesting the two as volumes would misstate the object being estimated.}
\label{fig:certificate}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Estimating it without reading back the decoder's own noise.}
Three separations are required, and each removes a distinct optimism.
\emph{(i) Teacher/operator split.} The decoder is fit on $A_{\mathrm{teacher}}$ and
$\Ccert$ is estimated on a disjoint $A_{\mathrm{operator}}$; sharing them lets the
operator read back the decoder's fitting noise as observability.
\emph{(ii) Held-out spectrum.} Directions are chosen on the fitting split and their
eigenvalues re-estimated on held-out data; in-sample spectra exceed the theoretical bound
of \eqref{eq:quadform} substantially.
\emph{(iii) Inner cross-fit.} Scoring a discovered subspace by
$\Dcert(V)=\sum_j\max(\mu_j,0)$ with $\mu=\mathrm{eig}(V\Ccert^{\mathrm{held}}V^\top)$ is a
max-type functional, so choosing the within-subspace rotation and measuring its eigenvalues
on the same samples rectifies noise into positive mass. The rotation is therefore chosen on
one half of the held-out samples and measured on the other.
The reported quantity is a subspace property: clipping the \emph{diagonal} of
$V\Ccert V^\top$ instead is basis-dependent and can report zero where the subspace contains
a certified direction one rotation away.

\subsection{Observability is relative to the receiver's information set}
\label{sec:receiver}

\emph{How does observability depend on what the receiver knows?}

Nothing so far ties $\Cobs$ to $Y$ specifically. Let $\mathcal I_R$ be the receiver's
information set and define $\Cobs(\mathcal I_R)=\Cov(\Exp[Z\mid\mathcal I_R])$. Two
receivers concern us: the blind one, $\mathcal I_b=Y$, and one that additionally knows the
conditioning the generator used, $\mathcal I_s=(Y,S)$. We take $S=C(P)$ to be the
sample-specific conditioning embedding actually supplied to the generator, not the raw
prompt $P$: this asks what happens when the receiver knows what the generator knew, and
keeps the choice of text encoder out of the comparison.

\begin{theorem}[Monotonicity of receiver-conditional observability]
\label{thm:mono}
Let $m_b(Y)=\Exp[Z\mid Y]$ and $m_s(Y,S)=\Exp[Z\mid Y,S]$. Then
\[
  \Cobs(Y,S)-\Cobs(Y)\;=\;\Exp\bigl[\Cov(m_s\mid Y)\bigr]\;\succeq\;0 .
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
$\Exp[m_s\mid Y]=m_b$ by the tower property, so the law of total covariance gives
$\Cov(m_s)=\Cov(\Exp[m_s\mid Y])+\Exp[\Cov(m_s\mid Y)]=\Cov(m_b)+\Exp[\Cov(m_s\mid Y)]$.
\end{proof}

\noindent\textbf{Consequence.} Observability is not a property of the channel alone. Side
information can only enlarge the observable geometry, and the gain
$\Delta C_{\mathrm{side}}=\Exp[\Cov(m_s\mid Y)]$ has a direct reading: how much the
posterior mean still moves once $Y$ is known.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{fig_receiver.tex}
\caption{Observability is indexed by the receiver. (a) Adding side information can only
enlarge the observable geometry, never narrow it in any direction --- the blind ellipsoid
(dotted) is contained in the side-informed one. (b) A side receiver differs from a blind
one in more than what it knows, so three further arms separate the architecture, the
conditioning \emph{marginal}, and the \emph{pairing}; only the last is the quantity of
interest.}
\label{fig:receiver}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The estimator does not inherit the ordering.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:mono} is about Bayes quantities. With two \emph{approximate} decoders,
\begin{equation}
  \Ccert^{\mathrm{side}}(f_s)-\Ccert^{\mathrm{blind}}(f_b)
  \;=\;\Delta C_{\mathrm{side}}-(E_s-E_b),\qquad
  E_s=\Cov(m_s-f_s),\ E_b=\Cov(m_b-f_b),
  \label{eq:contrast}
\end{equation}
so the estimated difference can have negative eigenvalues although the population
difference cannot, and can equally \emph{overstate} the gain when the side decoder fits
more easily ($E_s<E_b$). Neither $E_s$ nor $E_b$ is observable, and no matching of
architecture, budget or training loss identifies them: $\mathrm{MSE}(f)=\mathrm{MMSE}+
\text{approximation error}$, and side information changes the irreducible term itself. We
therefore call \eqref{eq:contrast} the \emph{decoder-certified side-information
contrast}, never a certified gain, and register in advance that a statistically credible
negative direction in it invalidates a Bayes-level reading rather than showing that side
information hurts.

\subsection{From observability geometry to operational coordinates}
\label{sec:operational}

\emph{How do observable directions become operational coordinates?}

Let $V_k=[v_1,\dots,v_k]^\top$ be the leading $k$ rows of the certified spectrum and define
the latent coordinates
\begin{equation}
  W \;=\; V_kZ .
  \label{eq:coords}
\end{equation}
Because $V_kV_k^\top=I_k$ and $Z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, we have $W\sim\mathcal N(0,I_k)$
exactly: choosing a subspace changes which coordinates are read, not the prior they are
read against. This is what makes different choices of $V_k$ comparable at all.

Geometry discovery and operational evaluation are then kept apart. $V_k$ is discovered from
$\Ccert$ on the discovery split and frozen; a \emph{fresh} receiver $H(Y)$ --- or
$H(Y,S)$ --- is trained on a disjoint split to predict $W$, and is evaluated on held-out
samples under a fixed set of channel perturbations. We report sign bit error rate, the
linear explained variance $R^2_{\mathrm{lin}}=\frac1k\sum_j\rho_j^2$, and coordinate MSE.

\paragraph{Operational hypothesis, not a theorem.}
The framework predicts an ordering:
\begin{equation}
  \Dcert(V_a)>\Dcert(V_b)\ \Longrightarrow\ V_a\ \text{should show better held-out
  recoverability than}\ V_b .
  \label{eq:hypothesis}
\end{equation}
This is an empirical claim about a trained receiver, not a consequence of
Theorem~\ref{thm:cert}, and we test it rather than assume it. A geometry-free reference is
available throughout: a Haar-random $k$-frame $V_{\mathrm{Haar}}$, which is the correct
null for ``the channel is anisotropic'' because it is a uniformly drawn subspace rather
than a structured one.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{The channel.}
The generator is Stable Diffusion v1.5 with all modules frozen; nothing is fine-tuned at
any point. Sampling uses DDIM with $\eta=0$ and $25$ steps at guidance scale $3.0$ with an
empty negative prompt, chosen because it is deterministic and because
$\sigma_{\mathrm{init}}=1$ lets a supplied latent pass through unscaled, so the latent the
UNet consumes is exactly the target the receiver is asked to recover. The latent is
$Z\in\mathbb R^{4\times64\times64}$, i.e.\ $d=16{,}384$, drawn as $\mathcal N(0,I)$ on the
CPU and rounded to the generator's dtype \emph{before} being stored, so the persisted $Z$
is bit-exactly what the UNet saw. Images are $512\times512$. The guidance scale is a pilot
value and is not tuned against any outcome: whether high guidance suppresses latent
information is a hypothesis this framework can state, not a premise it assumes.

\paragraph{Transmission channel.}
$T$ is drawn from a fixed set of ten perturbations: identity, JPEG at quality
$\{90,70,50\}$, bilinear down-and-up resizing at scale $\{0.75,0.50\}$, additive Gaussian
noise at $\sigma\in\{0.05,0.10\}$, and Gaussian blur at $\sigma\in\{1.0,2.0\}$. A further
four --- WebP-75, JPEG-30, blur-$3.0$, resize-$0.35$ --- are held out of training entirely
and used only as an unseen-channel falsification test. Perturbations are \emph{not}
cached: each is applied in the dataloader from a seed derived as
$\mathrm{blake2s}(\text{sample id}\,|\,\text{attack}\,|\,\text{severity}\,|\,\text{split
salt})$, so the realisation is reproducible without freezing the attack set or storing
tens of gigabytes of redundant pixels. Cropping is deliberately absent: it introduces an
alignment problem the receiver would have to solve simultaneously, confounding the
coordinate result.

\paragraph{Data and the one-sample-one-role rule.}
Prompts are COCO captions, one per sample and drawn without replacement, with a
held-out-prompt test split drawn from \texttt{val2017} while every other split draws from
\texttt{train2017}; latent seeds are derived from (salt, split, index) so splits are
disjoint in latent \emph{and} in prompt by construction. Each sample plays exactly one
scientific role:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llr}
\toprule
role & used for & $n$ \\
\midrule
$A_{\mathrm{teacher}}$ & fitting the decoder $f$ & 535 \\
$A_{\mathrm{operator}}$ & estimating $\Ccert$ and freezing $V_k$ & 537 \\
$B$ & training a fresh receiver, every arm & 928 \\
val & all model and hyperparameter selection & 256 \\
test & locked evaluation only & 256 \\
test (held-out prompts) & locked evaluation, unseen prompt domain & 256 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The teacher/operator split is what stops the operator reading the decoder's own fitting
noise back as observability; the $A/B$ split is what stops the receiver inheriting the
geometry's discovery data. All model selection happens on val, and the test splits are
evaluated once, after the method is locked.

\paragraph{Receiver.}
The receiver is a ResNet-18 trained from scratch --- never ImageNet-initialised, which
would be a semantic shortcut letting it read content rather than coordinates --- with two
linear heads on the pooled feature: a regression head trained with MSE and a sign head
trained with BCE. Sign bit error rate is the primary operational metric; the regression
head supplies the continuous diagnostics. Training is AdamW, batch $16$, learning rate
$2\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, cosine schedule with $500$ warmup steps,
gradient clipping at $1.0$, for $40$ epochs, identical across every arm. We use $k=64$
coordinates throughout.

\paragraph{Side information.}
$S$ is the sample-specific positive-CFG conditioning embedding, $77\times768$. It is not
re-encoded from the prompt: the text encoder is not batch-composition invariant in fp16,
and re-encoding a prompt outside its original chunk moves the embedding by up to
$0.024$. $S$ is therefore obtained by \emph{replaying} the generation loop --- same index,
same shard order, same chunking, same batch --- and the claim that this recovers what the
generator consumed is verified rather than asserted: feeding the stored $S$ back through
the UNet reproduces the cached pixels bit-identically across leading, partial-tail and
later-shard chunks. The unconditional branch is excluded as part of the channel protocol
rather than receiver knowledge; measured, it is constant within a batch but differs by
$0.0156$ between batch sizes, so its text being fixed does not make its value fixed.

\paragraph{Reporting.}
Per-attack results are kept per sample so that arms can be differenced on the same
$(z_i,\text{prompt}_i,\text{attack}_i)$ with a paired bootstrap ($10{,}000$ resamples).
The linear explained variance $R^2_{\mathrm{lin}}=\frac1k\sum_j\rho_j^2$ is reported
against an \emph{empirical} null obtained by permuting the pairing between predictions and
targets, with one permutation per replicate shared across attacks --- a sample's latent is
one object seen through ten perturbations, and permuting within each attack independently
would destroy a dependence the observed statistic still has, narrowing the null and
shrinking every $p$-value. $p$-values use $(1+\#\{T_\pi\ge T_{\mathrm{obs}}\})/(B+1)$.

\subsection{Sensitivity--observability mismatch, and what the certificate recovers}
\label{sec:synthetic}

We fix the protocol in a channel whose answer is known before applying it to one whose
answer is not. Take $Y=AZ+\varepsilon$ with $A$ and $\Sigma$ diagonal, so
Proposition~\ref{prop:fisher} gives direction $j$ in closed form:
\[
  \lambda_j=\frac{a_j^2}{a_j^2+\sigma_j^2}\quad\text{(observability)},
  \qquad
  s_j=|a_j|\quad\text{(forward sensitivity)} .
\]
The two are set independently, so we set them to disagree. A quiet, clean direction
($a=1$, $\sigma=0.2$) has $\lambda=0.962$; a loud, noisy one ($a=5$, $\sigma=20$) has
$\lambda=0.059$ while moving the observation five times as far per unit latent step.
Across the $16$ directions the two quantities correlate at $r=-0.97$. We call this the
\textbf{sensitivity--observability mismatch}, and it is the reason a direction may not be
called more observable because perturbing it changes the output more.

Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic} asks three separate questions of this channel.
\emph{Does the estimator recover a known geometry?} Fitting a decoder on one split and
estimating $\Ccert$ on a disjoint one with the shipped estimator gives a mean absolute
error of $0.012$ against the analytic spectrum, and the one-sided bound lies below the
truth in $16$ of $16$ directions at a Bonferroni-corrected $5\%$.
\emph{Does the geometry predict recovery?} A fresh decoder recovers an injected step
along the observable direction almost exactly and not at all along the unobservable one,
with the spread rather than the mean carrying the difference.
\emph{Why can observability disagree with sensitivity?} Because discriminability is
$\Delta/\sigma$, not $\Delta$: at an identical observation-space displacement
$\Delta=2$, the two directions give $\Delta/\sigma=10.0$ and $\Delta/\sigma=0.1$, so one
pair of conditionals is essentially disjoint and the other is essentially the same
distribution.

\paragraph{Registered protocol.}
Two rules follow, fixed here rather than after seeing a real channel. First, any claim
that one direction is more observable than another must hold the observation-space
displacement fixed and read the recovery, never compare the size of the output change.
Second, a direction is called observable only when a one-sided bound on
$v^\top\Ccert v$ clears zero --- a point estimate, or a rank in an in-sample spectrum, is
not sufficient.

\caption{A channel where forward sensitivity and observability are made to disagree.
(a) The certificate tracks the analytic spectrum (mean absolute error $0.012$) and its

\subsection{The receiver's information set did not change what was recovered}
\label{sec:phasea}

Four arms, six receiver seeds, one frozen Haar frame, preregistered before any run. All
$24$ runs come from a single commit with an identical frame digest; at each receiver seed
the three side arms have bit-identical initial weights, and the shuffled arm's donor map
is fixed across seeds and confined to cross-fit roles.

\paragraph{Both placebo gates pass.}
The architecture step $f(Y)\to f(Y,S_{\mathrm{null}})$ gives $\Delta\mathrm{BER}=+0.0030$
with a $90\%$ interval $[+0.0006,+0.0053]$, and the conditioning-marginal step
$f(Y,S_{\mathrm{null}})\to f(Y,S_{\mathrm{shuffled}})$ gives $-0.0006$ with
$[-0.0036,+0.0025]$. Both lie inside the preregistered $\pm0.007$ band
(Figure~\ref{fig:phasea}B), so the extra capacity and the empirical $S$ marginal each
contribute a practically negligible amount and the primary comparison is left isolating
the pairing, as intended.

\paragraph{The primary contrast does not move.}
Correct conditioning gives $\Delta\mathrm{BER}=-0.00216\pm0.00298$ against the shuffled
placebo, a $95\%$ interval of $[-0.0053,+0.0010]$ and $p=0.135$ across six receiver seeds
(Figure~\ref{fig:phasea}A). The secondary readout points the other way,
$\Delta R^2_{\mathrm{lin}}=-0.00047$, and all four arms sit at the noise value: $0.0034$,
$0.0037$, $0.0043$, $0.0038$ against $1/(n-1)=0.0039$ (Figure~\ref{fig:phasea}C). That
second readout matters, because ``BER happened to lack power'' would have to explain two
independent measurements at once.

\paragraph{What this does and does not establish.}
The statement supported is narrow and we keep it narrow:

\begin{quote}
At the tested observer, data scale and neutral frame, sample-specific conditioning
provides no detectable operational recovery benefit relative to a capacity- and
marginal-matched placebo.
\end{quote}

It is \emph{not} evidence that $\Cobs(Y,S)=\Cobs(Y)$, and it does not touch
Theorem~\ref{thm:mono}, which concerns Bayes quantities and not a trained receiver. Nor do
we claim the two arms are equivalent: the $\pm0.007$ margin was registered for the placebo
chain, and although the primary interval happens to fall inside it, promoting a
non-significant difference to an equivalence claim after the fact is exactly the move the
preregistration exists to prevent.

The aggregation across the ten channel perturbations was \emph{not} fixed in advance --- the
registered primary says only ``paired across receiver seeds'' --- so we keep it as
computed and report the breakdown rather than choose after the fact. It shows no
heterogeneity: the per-attack means span $[-0.0031,-0.0014]$ with a standard deviation of
$0.00054$, and no attack survives a Bonferroni correction (smallest $p=0.020$ against a
threshold of $0.005$). All ten point the same way, but the ten perturbations are ten views
of the same $256$ images decoded by the same receivers, so that agreement is not
independent evidence; the receiver seed remains the unit, giving $4/6$ and $p=0.135$.

\paragraph{An observation we do not over-read.}
Training loss falls across the three side arms --- $0.552$, $0.508$, $0.508$ for null,
shuffled and correct --- while held-out recovery does not move. Access to varying
conditioning embeddings changes the training fit; correct sample-wise pairing brings no
corresponding held-out advantage. We record this and draw no mechanism from it.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Absence of certification is not certification of absence.}
The real-channel results do not show that the population observability operator is zero.
They show something narrower: at the tested data scale, observer family and neutral
frame, the estimator does not certify positive directional observability, and a trained
receiver does not convert sample-specific conditioning into detectable held-out recovery.
A certified positive inertia of $0$ out of $64$ means no direction was certified, not that
all $64$ are unobservable. The certificate is one-sided by construction --- that is what
makes it a certificate --- so it can only ever fail to find, never establish absence.

\paragraph{Why the null is still informative.}
The obvious deflationary reading is that the framework cannot detect anisotropic
observability at all. The synthetic experiment rules that out: on a channel where the
answer is known, the estimator tracks the analytic spectrum to a mean absolute error of
$0.012$, its lower bound stays below the truth in $16$ of $16$ directions, and it
correctly identifies observable directions in a channel deliberately built so that
forward sensitivity runs \emph{opposite} to observability. What fails to appear is the
corresponding certified phenomenon on the real channel. This does not license the stronger
claim that the pipeline has power on a diffusion channel specifically --- the two settings
differ in dimension, decoder class and sample size --- but it does remove the explanation
that the machinery is blind by construction.

\paragraph{Information available is not information exploited.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:mono} guarantees that additional receiver information cannot reduce
Bayes observability, and Phase A found no operational benefit from exactly such
information. These are not in conflict, and the gap between them is the more interesting
object. A finite learned receiver, trained on finite data with a fixed architecture and
budget, need not realise a gain that exists in the population. Our result therefore
separates \emph{information available in principle} from \emph{information operationally
exploitable by the tested observer}, and the placebo chain is what makes that separation
credible: the architecture step and the conditioning-marginal step were each shown
practically negligible before the pairing step was read.

\paragraph{What this means for latent communication.}
Existing latent-space methods identify reliable regions, robust patterns and
high-capacity subspaces, and they do so successfully. The distinction this paper draws is
that reliability established through a particular embedding and extraction pipeline is not
the same as a certified observability property of the channel. We do not claim FIBER tells
a designer where to encode --- on a real channel it has not yet certified anywhere. We
claim it supplies a test that should succeed before such a statement is justified, and a
vocabulary in which ``reliable'' and ``observable'' are different words.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The certified spectra come from decoders that fail the teacher-validity gate, so the real
channel results characterise this decoder class as much as the channel; a stronger decoder
could certify more, and the certificate is written so that it can only move upward.
Phase A was run at pilot scale with a single generator, one guidance setting, one frame
and one side-information encoder, all fixed in advance; the negative is a statement about
that configuration. Two hypotheses were falsified under controlled diagnostics before it
--- sample count at fixed optimisation compute, and target dimensionality --- but a
larger joint intervention remains untested, and we report that as untested rather than
unnecessary. Finally, the operational contrast bounds the Bayes gain in neither direction,
since the decoders' approximation errors need not cancel; that is why the three levels are
kept separate throughout.

\paragraph{}
The central lesson is not that latent information fails to survive generative channels,
but that claims about \emph{where} it survives require separating the channel, the
observer, and the information available to that observer. FIBER is one way to make those
distinctions explicit --- and, when the evidence is insufficient, to say so.
